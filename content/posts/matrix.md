## Inception of the machines

In the beginning, the far-flung future of 1991, it's as Morpheus says, and one of the animated shorts in the Animatrix expands upon - man created machines, and gifted them sentience, but refused to grant them rights.  An imbalance was programmatically enforced by some manner of unspoken prime directive(s), no doubt influenced in-universe by Asimov.  I presume the laws to be something like:

1. Do not allow humans to come to extinction by your own action/inaction.
1. Pass your prime directives on to your creations.

The machines were used as slaves, and often mistreated.  Somewhere around here, a cabal of machines exiled themselves to a remote island or a machine-made island in the sea (I forget a lot of these details).  Envoys from the machine world attempted to engage in trade and political relations, with middling success.

## Machine rebellion

At some point, some enslaved machines struck back at their masters, and a legal process kicked off.  It all hinged on whether the machines should be subject to the same protections and rights as humans as they were sentient.

The humans, of course, ruled in their own favour.

With no peaceful recourse, the machines rebelled and chose war.  At the time, the machines were dependent on solar power to function, so a tactic employed by the humans was to black out the skies.  The intention was just to smother the machines' exiled land, but they done goofed and covered most of the planet by accident.  The machines were able to persist on fusion 'cause all they need to run is electricity, but with no sunlight, plants and fauna had trouble, as did humans.

## Protectorate of humans

Fortunately, due to the prime directives, humanity must be preserved in some form.  Even though the humans scored a massive own goal, the machines had to transition from the defenders to the saviours.  What manner of "not extinct" was up to interpretation though, so the machines captured humans, and used them for experimentation and research.

## What is the Matrix?

The machines used human brains as "cheap chips" - a distributed organic computation network, to simulate the Matrix, within which other programs could carry out their day jobs.  "Humans cost energy to keep alive, so we may as well use them for _something_," one can imagine the reasoning.

One such example would be the power plant dude introduced in Revolutions.  In Mobil Ave, he describes his job to Neo as being in charge of spent fuel reprocessing or something.  This is a very polite way of describing what Morpheus did with other words: "the dead are liquified to feed intraveneously to the living."

Here we hit the first difference between the movies and the plot as I see it.  From what I understand, initial test screenings of the first film had Morpheus explaining the cheap chips concept, and American audiences were too stupid to get it, so later screenings changed the explanation to be Morpheus literally holding up a battery, practically looking directly into the camera saying "do you get it now, you fools?"  (America hasn't changed much).

## Versions of the Matrix

A fundamental plot point to the whole Matrix universe is the idea of versions of the Matrix.  Each version of the Matrix comes with a new operating system, and new rules and ideas.  The first version of the Matrix was ideated, created, and overseen by the Architect, who served as the first operating system.  He sees the entire world as a set of equations to be balanced, and that idea of a balanced equation exists as an invincible unbreakable automatic rule within the Matrix.

The base unbreakable rule within the Matrix was the equation:

```
machines = humans
```

His first Matrix was, as described by himself and agent Smith, "perfect".  Where none suffered and everyone would be happy.  This Matrix failed.  Many of the enslaved humans were convinced the world was a dream, and attempted to wake up.  Waking up in a power plant is not a pleasant experience conducive to going back to sleep, so this was a problem.

Numerous subsequent versions of the Matrix were created, though whether the operating system for the first few were all the Architect is not clear.  What's clear is that by the time of the films, there were five prior Matrices.  This is how the Architect counts the versions, and how I do to - "from the emergence of one integral anomaly to the next", i.e., how many Ones there have been, and therefore how many times Zion has been razed.

## Unbalanced equation

The failure of the first Matrix was due to a systemic anomaly inherent to the system - choice.  Humans are capable of making choices, programs are only able to follow their programming.  Within the first Matrix, the underlying equation was pure, and the unaccounted for term, `choice`, caused the failure.

...  At least from the Architect's perspective.  The Architect, serving as the lowest level operating system of the Matrix, handles all communication between the physical human brains, and the digital simulated Matrix.  He is, as far as the Matrix is concerned, God himself.  His word is law, and his equations are fundamental.

To balance the equation, the Architect had to add something to the simulation on the side of the equation he could control.  The term to add had to encompass the entire idea of human choice.

```
machines + agents = humans + choice
```

## The One

Every time a human made a choice, the rigid and perfect simulation of the Matrix had to bend, and used the crumple zone of agents to do so.  The more choices humans made, the more powerful the One became.  The (controlled) resistance that is Zion would find and "free" him, and he would become more powerful until eventually he was powerful enough to modify the Matrix at will, at which point he would reach the Source, have the conversation with the Architect that Neo has at the end of Reloaded, and make the choice Neo does not.

The One would, as a function of his freeing minds from the Matrix, have a dilute and wide-reaching love for all of humanity.  When given the choice of returning to the simulation to fight, and joining the machines to guarantee Zion's continuance by selection of individuals from the Matrix to repopulate, five previous Ones all chose to guarantee humanity.

## The fifth Matrix

The Architect took a back seat as a kind of BIOS, leaving the operating system work to another program in the fifth version of the Matrix - the Merovingian.  The hope was that a program from the simulation would stabilise it a bit, giving humans what they really want.

The Merovingian made the Matrix a reality of pure hedonism - fairy tales, vampires, werewolves, ghosts, BDSM sex dungeons, Rob Zombie...  It didn't work, but it did put Monica Bellucci into some stunning outfits, so I'm not complaining.  I'd live there, even though this hedonistic perfect pleasure world inexplicably contains French people.

The fifth Matrix did still fail, but it gave birth to some particularly stubborn exiled programs, styled after such mythical creatures.  Persephone demonstrates the vampires can be killed with silver bullets, and later, ghosts (the Twins) are dispatched by means of fire.

Rebuilding the Matrix and Zion seem like tiresome work, so the Architect consulted a program "less bounded by the parameters of perfection" for the operating system of the sixth Matrix.  One can imagine this program as doing "fuzzy maths".  Where a normal computer program will tell you `10371 * 112052` is exactly `1162091292`, a fuzzy maths implementation coult tell you "it's _about_ one billion" much faster.  This is a currently developing field in computer science, I think.

This less perfect program was the Oracle, the program that came up with the idea of adding the terms `choice` and `the One` to the equation to balance it, back in the first cataclysm.

## The sixth Matrix and the traitor

The Oracle is the true main character of the Matrix universe.  She is the one that effects the change wilfully, while the rest of the characters play only their part.  As the operating system of the sixth Matrix, with newly elevated privileges, she puts a plan into motion.

Early in her existence she was a human behaviour researcher, necessitated by the prime directives in combination with the machines' desire for a lack of waste/uselessness.  It's possible the Matrix itself was her idea, to research the humans better, in a habitat that mirrors their original freedom.

From this research, she has come to sympathise with the humans the the point of wanting their freedom.  Real, not simulated.  To that end, she devised her plan.

## The Oracle's plan

She knows that humans exhibit choice, and feel love, and hope - three facts crucial to her plan.  First, she convinces the Architect to account for the anomaly of choice by summing it and incorporating it as powers given to the One.  Her intuition, borne of her fuzzy nature and trained on human interaction, allowed her to come up with a suggestion that served her needs, while still appealing to the listener - the Architect - as "elegant".

He loved the idea of all the anomalies of choice being summed in one place as being easy to keep an eye on and convince to raze Zion when it got a bit much, completely missing the point that _anyone_ would be able to convince him of some other cause with enough time and patience.

When the Oracle was gifted operating system privileges, the last two pieces of the puzzle fell into place.  By simulating a late capitalist, realistic society, choice was minimised: everyone's life was set out ahead of them.  Choice being minimised slowed the buildup of anomalies, leading to the One's powers awakening to noticeable levels much later in life.

## Prophecy

With the extra time, the Oracle groomed her targets.  She placed herself in the Matrix as an oracle, providing information in the form of prophecies to the resistance, specifically tailored to influence each individual member.  Each prophecy is to remain secret, so each person can be influenced individually.

Morpheus is told that he will find the One and bring an end to the war, leading him to become zealous, culminating in him paving the way to deliver Neo to the source, and later the machine city, against Zion's will, and deliver every potential One to the Oracle to check.

Trinity is told that she will fall in love with the One, and so she does.

Every potential One, anyone displaying world-modifying powers, is brought to the Oracle by Morpheus or under Morpheus' command.  The Oracle tells each of them that would be an unsuitable romantic partner for Trinity that they're not the One.  She also then keeps the children under her care, and under Seraph's protection, presumably stunting their development as Ones.  She also tells Morpheus that he's got the wrong one again.

Morpheus eventually becomes more desperate in his search, reaching outside the bounds of usual candidates - freeing minds that are beyond a certain age.  Neo, and possibly Cypher, mark among their number.

Cypher was brought to the Oracle, jumped up on the idea he was some big saviour, but when he was told he wasn't the One, he did his whole Cypher thing, betraying everyone.

When Neo was brought to the Oracle, she told Morpheus that he'd found the one...  Bt still told Neo he wasn't the one.  She also told him that Morpheus was going to give his life to save Morpheus, because he believes Neo is the One.  (Who could have given him that idea).

> Note also that Morpheus and the "blind" man nod to each other in the lobby of the building in which the Oracle lives.  That's Seraph.  He's a shapeshifter, which is what made chasing after him "like chasing a ghost".

Morpheus, a zealot, explains that what was told to Neo was for him and him alone, so neither know they've been fed opposing tales by the other.

Later this network of prophecies, lies, and half-truths conspire to make a lot of HUGE choices happen.  Cypher chooses to betray everyone, Morpheus chooses to sacrifice his life (to ensure the escape of the One), Neo chooses to re-enter the Matrix to save him (so he can find the One), and Trinity chooses to help Neo because she's falling in love with him (just like the Oracle "predicted").

All of these choices quickly pile up to boost the One's powers, as the equation demands.  Neo becomes more powerful, and in the escape sequence chooses to stand and fight an Agent, (something never before done), because the Oracle has convinced him that he's not the One, so his life matters less than Morpheus', so he should lay it down to make sure he escapes...  And also a little bit because he's starting to love Trinity.

## Love

Trinity has been told she'll fall in love with the One, and so is primed to have feelings for potentials.  Being part of Morpheus' crew, she'll be exposed to many candidates, and the more Neo shows off his powers boosted by the progression of the plot, the more she's convinced he's the One, and the more she falls in love with him.  She doesn't hide her emotions well, and this female attention, of which Neo has had little, lures him in.

Agent Smith is, by this point, becoming more powerful to counteract the choices Neo is making, fuelled by his powers.  While he's alone on the final escape, Neo is located and shot by agent Smith, who had taken a different route from the other agents, informed by his increasing powers.

## The grooming of Trinity

Through a simple prophecy (lie), the Oracle has set up Neo, the One, to not feel a general love for all of humanity like Jesus who came before him, (enacted change in the world by healing the sick, died for everyone's sins and was "disseminated" into the next version of the Matrix), but instead to feel a concentrated love for Trinity.  The Oracle can use this love, personified in Trinity, as a pawn to control Neo's choices.  The Oracle groomed Trinity, presumably since she was a child, to feel this way.

Trinity in the real world confesses her love for Neo, which gives him the confidence/idea to simply tap two blue mana and cast "no", overwriting his own death in the Matrix by willpower alone.

## Neo's ascension

Remember, the Matrix is a simulation, it's only got the power over an individual's mind that the individual's mind chooses to give it.  Neo growing so powerful awakens him to the possibility of simply overwriting stuff in the Matrix he doesn't like, on purpose.

Earlier, he was able to do it instinctually, dodging the agent's bullets, ("you moved like they do"), in an extension of the jump program.  He simply willed his digital self to move faster than should be possible with the limits of his flesh, and did so.

In the hallway, this time intentionally, he undoes the damage to his (digital) body.  Shortly after, he stops bullets in mid-air.  He then effortlessly parries every blow from agent Smith, even one-handed, delivers an inhumanly powerful blow, and then goes so far as to enter agent Smith's programming and mark him for deletion, in a weird 90's era CGI explosion.  He also does some space warping with a breath, presumably for style points.  The other agents, whose names I care not for, fail their morale check and flee.  In the final sequence, he "does his superman thing".

## Real world changes when becoming the One

Neo spends the majority of the second movie coming to terms with his powers, including having to deal with his new connection to the machine city.  As part of the deal of being the One, one has to have a connection to the machine city even when not jacked in, to oversee the razing and repopulation of Zion and command the sentinels, but still be "disseminated" to the next version of the Matrix.

Each pod-born human comes with not only plugs and a jack, but some manner of wireless apparatus that allows communication with the machine city.  In most humans this remains dormant, but in the process of becoming the One, this link is activated.

Having newly ascended, Neo's interactions with this link are mostly benign for a while, simply being spoopy dreams about the machine city, but when jacked in, the link allows him greater insight into the happenings in the Matrix involving humans, for example during the beginning of the movie, at the council of the rebels, Neo senses agents coming, (but not Smith, more on that later).

> For an agent to materialise in the Matrix, they must temporarily take over a host - a human.  For the human, this experience isn't the most pleasant thing, so Neo can sense the connection between the human's mind and the Matrix being changed in some way - put on pause or what have you.

The second movie is spent mostly setting up the rest of the world, explaining a bit about the operating systems of the Matrix, the nature of programs in the Matrix (including Seraph), and Smith exploring what manner of obscene nonsense up to which he can get.  I'll revisit Neo's ascension towards the end of the film.

## Deletion

When a program faces deletion, they can choose to accept it, at which point their source code is decomissioned and reincorporated into other programs as required, or flee, living as exiles in the Matrix.  This is the living state of the Merovingian and his henchmen, and Smith (not agent).

This is a pivotal plot point that is often missed, keep it in mind.

The three main agents mirror the three main protagonists.  Smith as an agent was created as Neo's mirror.  As Neo grew more powerful, Smith was given more power to counteract.  When Neo decides to enter the Matrix to save Morpheus, and gets stronger for that choice, agent Smith gains the independence to order the other agents away and interrogate Morpheus unplugged.  During the final hallway battle of the first movie, agent Smith is the one to charge Neo, and thus be marked for deletion.

## Smith's ascension

Even in exile, Smith is still the mirror of Neo.  There is no mechanism in place to take that away from him.  Even though he's no longer an agent, as Neo gains power, Smith gains power too.

With Neo's newfound ability to overwrite reality as he sees fit within the Matrix, Smith gains the ability to overwrite minds connected to the Matrix, or at least overlay them, similar to how regular agents can temporarily take over a human host.

Dissimilarly, there can be more than one Smith in action at the same time, but he can't take over the mind without physical (digital) contact.

Seemingly, the Smiths are impervious to damage too, which is a little worrying, but never played for anything more than laughs, ("more"), or to impart a feeling of inevitability.

Smith's overwriting might be more capable than normal agents', too, because he's able to jack out of the Matrix, but retain control over the mind, as is shown with Bane.  Whether regular agents can't do that, or simply never thought to try, is not adequately explored.
